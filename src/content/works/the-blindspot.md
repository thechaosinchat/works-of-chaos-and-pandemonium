title: "The Blindspot"

The fog rolling up against the hallway windows was thick, suffocating, and immensely bleak. It was the kind of dampness that crawled under your collar and stayed there, making you feel disgusting for the entire day. The weather alone was enough to piss him off. Or maybe it was the fact that he had to attend school that day. Either way, his shoulders locked up, bracing for the crushing boredom of a world he wished didn’t exist.

A locker door slammed shut next to him, jolting him awake. He fiddled with his locker dial lock, tugging at the locker door with all his frustration. He turned around, banging the back of his head onto the locker, and looked down at the floor towards his scuffed Vans, defeated and still very much annoyed.

Until he saw a glint of light next to his foot.

It was right there on the scuffed linoleum. A single, tiny key that was way out of place, yet nobody had noticed. And it was placed right in front of his locker. He bent over and scooped it up with extreme care, treating the light brass as a sunken treasure. The stamped engraving on the key read: SECURITY.

His heart skipped a beat. Everyone knew the rumor of the sub-basement security room. It was a vintage relic filled with buzzing CRT screens and time-lapse VCRs from the late nineties that the school was too broke to upgrade. They chose to keep the analog gear running in the dark until two years ago, when parents threw a massive fit over student privacy and forced the board to pull the plug on the system, and now the room was off limits until they do renovations.

For him and him only, it was the invitation he’d been waiting for. He’d been going into abandoned or restricted areas since he was 12 and he was running out of new areas to explore. Dead, forgotten spots were the only places where he felt completely in control, free from expectations, people, and society in general.

English class was in two minutes. Skipping it would mean detention, but it wasn’t even a debate. He pocketed the brass key, turned his back on the crowd heading towards classrooms, and headed to the fabled security room.

“Got a hall pass?” Some teacher called out. His shoulders instinctively locked tight. He shoved his hands in his pockets and walked faster, pretending not to hear him. Recognizing him, the teacher just gave a forlorn sigh and went back to his business.

The corridors grew steadily quieter the further he walked away from the main academic wing. The fluorescent lights hummed overhead, casting long shadows against the lockers until the polished tile gave way to gray concrete. At the very end of a dead-end hallway sat a windowless metal door. The room label next to the door had been removed, leaving nothing but four empty nail holes.

He pulled the key from his pocket, his pulse quickening. The brass slid into the tumbler, catching for a split second before turning with a heavy, satisfying *clack*. He pushed his shoulder against the steel and slipped inside.

His hand traced the dark, damp, cinderblock wall until his fingers brushed a toggle switch. He flicked it. A row of caged bulbs buzzed violently to life, casting a sickly yellow glare down a steep wooden staircase. The air blowing up from the dark was bone-dry and cold, carrying the distinct metallic scent of ozone and undisturbed dust.

He descended slowly, the wooden steps groaning under his scuffed Vans. His pulse pounded in his ears.

At the bottom, he stepped onto an old-fashioned checkered linoleum floor. The room was a windowless bunker, with cobwebs stretching like netting between exposed iron pipes with gray concrete walls enclosing him on all sides.

Dead center stood a heavy wooden desk cluttered with scattered papers, a rusted fan, and the legacy system itself: four large, curved-glass CRT monitors stacked over a bulky VCR deck. The entire setup was blanketed in a thick layer of gray fur.

He wiped a clean streak across the main monitor and pulled the squeaky desk drawer. It screeched in protest as he yanked it open, revealing perfectly organized, dust-caked rows of VHS tapes. He dragged his finger across the plastic spines, stopping on one labeled in faded black marker: ROOM-112 14-10-01.

He instantly remembered that date.

Sophomore year. Honors Algebra 1. He remembered sitting in the back row — as always — with his sketchbook open, the rhythmic chewing of his watermelon gum the only thing keeping him awake. The teacher had suddenly stopped in her lecture and called his name, catching him off guard.

In his mind, he was the nonchalant protagonist. He remembered the entire class turning to look at him. He remembered slouching in his chair, offering a slow, careless shrug and replying smoothly “I don’t know. Go ask Steven or something.”

He remembered the silence that followed, interpreting it as awe. He had used Steven — the nervous, overachieving kid at the front of the class — as a prop to prove he didn’t care about the class or the social hierarchy. He was above it all.

Looking through the drawer, he wiped the dust off of another tape. FLOOR1-HALL 6-1-01. He remembered this too, much more clearly than the first.

It was in the first floor hallways right when school ended. He remembered it being a cold winter and he was rushing to go to the bus when he realized that the girl from his English class was waving to him. He remembered that the girl had a huge crush on him and that everyone in the school knew. He remembered quickly processing the situation, and recalling the cynical advice his old middle school friends had given him. A girlfriend was a “bad investment” and they would just “ruin his mental health”. So, in his mind, he handled it perfectly. He had nonchalantly pretended not to notice her, keeping his gaze dead ahead, and calmly walked past her to catch his bus. Just another flawless victory for the junior.

He set the second tape on the desk and turned his attention back to the security system.

He pressed the power button and the monitors flared to life with a loud burst of static. He pushed the first tape into the VCR machine and pressed play, causing the main monitor to settle into a grainy, top-down, black-and-white feed of Room 112.

There was no audio, which didn’t surprise him. Recording audio of children was probably illegal. He leaned back in the squeaky wooden chair, crossing his arms, a smirk creeping across his face as he saw his sophomore self sitting in the back row from the front of the classroom, at least ten feet off the ground. He waited for the teacher to call on him. He wanted to see his own effortless bravado.

But as the silent footage played, his smirk faded.

He leaned forward, grabbing the cold plastic sides of the monitor, squinting at the flickering glass. That couldn’t be right.

The kid on the screen wasn’t slouching smoothly, but rather entirely rigid. His shoulders were hiked up past his collarbone. Before the teacher even spoke, that kid was constantly darting his eyes around the room, frantically scanning the other students to gauge if anyone was looking at him. Underneath his desk, his foot was tapping anxiously, a textbook reaction of pure stress.

When the teacher finally called his name, the kid on the screen didn’t look apathetic at all. He looked terrified. The casual shrug he remembered was actually a jerky, defensive spasm. He watched himself snap a response to the teacher, and, although there was no sound, the aggressive thrust of his chin made him look small, fragile, and desperate to be left alone.

He wasn’t sitting on his high throne, but rather on the floor, kneeling with the peasants.

A heavy, sickening weight sank in his stomach. He felt a profound wave of disappointment wash over him. That effortless mask he thought he spent years perfecting didn’t even exist. He let out a long, shaky sigh, his chest tightening as he reached out and hit the eject button. The machine spat out his failures with a mechanical hiss.

He grabbed the second tape, hoping it was better than the first.

He immediately noticed it. The moment the girl waved at him, his entire body tensed. He quickly swiveled his head to look away, his shoulders locking up all over again. When he finally looked back at her, he walked past, exaggeratedly swinging his shoulders and shoving some nearby kids, all staring daggers into him.

Why were his shoulders so tense? Have they always been so tense? He wondered as he finally noticed himself. His short and shallow breaths, his foot constantly tapping and tapping and tapping. It was exhausting. Like wading through a pool of mud, he noticed that his mind had been completely clogged. He never gave any real care to what he did; he simply reacted, losing himself in his own delusions, losing himself in his own imagination. Sitting in the dark with nothing but himself and the faint humming of the monitor, he finally noticed the errors of his ways: the fragile egoist inside him and the permeative fog in his mind.

A rush of echoing footsteps above him forced him out of his daze. Dismissal time. The junior looked around, returned the tapes to the drawer, powered down the system, then walked back into that first floor hallway, like he did with every dismissal.

The hallway wasn’t packed with kids as he thought there’d be, with the exception of a few smart kids lingering by doors, talking to teachers. He walked past them, head down, eager to get out of the building.

SLAM!! His train of thought was derailed by a sharp thud. He looked over his shoulder to see a kid tripping over himself, his backpack hitting the linoleum and spilling a landslide of crumpled papers onto the floor.

He hesitated for a moment. Then he turned back around to leave, following his instincts. He continued for another step, before he noticed the familiar ache in his shoulders.

Again? He thought, trying to shake it off.

He attempted to take another step, trying to ignore the tension in his muscles, but he refused to let himself operate on auto-pilot again. He stopped in his tracks, took a slow, controlled deep breath, and turned around to face the kid.

It was Steven.

Steven caught the junior looking at him and immediately flinched, turning away to frantically shove pages of notes into his backpack. The junior hesitated for another moment, and then, he let it go.

He physically forced his arms to drop. That rigid armor he’d worn for years faded away, leaving his arms hanging loose by his sides. He calmly walked towards Steven and crouched down on the floor, and started putting together binders and notebooks.

When he handed over the stack of biology notes, neatly tucked into the green folder, Steven stared at him like a complete stranger. And in a way, he was. The junior didn’t smirk, didn’t check if anyone was watching, and was more relaxed than Steven had ever seen before.

As the junior walked out the double doors into the bright afternoon sun, the fog had lifted. He felt more relaxed than he had ever been, and he noticed that his shoulders were as well.

Senior year was going to be different. He was finally in control.
